\abstract{
\textbf{Motivation:} Benchmarks for DNA sequence classification underpin method comparison, but their train/test splits rarely control for near-duplicate sequences spanning the split, letting a model score well by recognizing memorized near-copies rather than by generalizing. Whether this merely inflates scores, or changes \emph{which} method appears best, has not been characterized on a widely used suite.\\
\textbf{Results:} We audit the seven binary datasets of Genomic Benchmarks: two are leaky, four clean under both a $k$-mer and a length-robust containment metric, and one borderline. A near-duplicate-aware re-split lowers the best model's accuracy by $11.8$ points on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} and $16.5$ on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}; cluster-bootstrap intervals exclude zero, and every clean-dataset delta includes zero. On \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} the leading model changes under accuracy, AUROC and $F_1$ alike, and under a chromosome-holdout control. Across a nine-learner roster the leaky split cannot separate its top three---a multilayer perceptron, $1$-nearest-neighbour and extremely randomized trees finish within $0.0015$---yet those three span $23$ points once corrected; $1$-nearest-neighbour falls from rank~2 to last, $0.873$ to $0.537$. On \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} the demotion is accuracy-only, so the reordering is demonstrated on one dataset and cautionary on the other. The cause is a curation defect we can manipulate: the leaky positive class ships $77{,}421$ intervals on only $40{,}934$ distinct coordinates, and editing coordinates alone abolishes the reordering and manufactures it in a clean dataset. Three of eleven independent Nucleotide Transformer tasks are similarly leaky---one at $25\%$ byte-identical---and six more borderline, though no ranking there inverts materially. A pre-registered prediction that GUE's short human regulatory tasks would be leaky failed: all eleven are clean at full scale, so leakage tracks how a dataset was assembled rather than what kind of sequence it contains.\\
\textbf{Availability and implementation:} Code, the report card, and a dependency-free near-duplicate-aware splitter and certification tool are at \url{https://github.com/rikhinkavuru/homology-leakage-genomic-benchmarks}. The classical audit is CPU-only and deterministic.}

\keywords{data leakage, near-duplicate sequences, benchmark evaluation, genomics, model ranking}

\section{Introduction}

Machine learning for DNA sequence classification is now routine, and benchmark suites have become the default proving grounds for new methods. The Genomic Benchmarks collection \citep{gresova2023} is widely used precisely because it is small, accessible, and quick to run, making it a natural first stop when comparing models. The scientific value of any such suite, however, rests entirely on one assumption: that its train/test split measures generalization rather than recall of the training data.

Near-duplicate leakage threatens this assumption. We use \emph{near-duplicate leakage} to mean the situation in which exact or near-identical sequences appear on both sides of a split; where local-alignment or containment evidence earns the stronger word, we call the shared-ancestry variant \emph{homology}. A model can then succeed on the test set by recognizing memorized near-copies of training examples rather than by learning generalizable signal, so reported accuracy overstates true generalization. This failure mode is increasingly documented across biological machine learning, from regulatory-genomics regression to protein-interaction prediction and virtual screening (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

Two claims must be kept separate, and only the second is ours. \textbf{Claim~II}---that near-duplicate leakage can \emph{reorder} which model appears best---is our contribution, and it is scoped: it requires a memorization-prone model in the comparison set and a split whose construction leaves near-duplicates spanning it. Both datasets on which we demonstrate it are short human regulatory sets---one fixed-length at $251$\,bp, the other variable at a $269$\,bp median over a $2$--$573$ range---so we cannot rule that regime out as a further necessary condition; but it is certainly not a sufficient one, and \S\ref{sec:r-census} reports eleven short human regulatory tasks---seven of them independent---pre-registered by us as leaky, that are clean. We demonstrate Claim~II cleanly on one dataset and, honestly, only partially on a second. \textbf{Claim~I}---that de-leaking a split lowers apparent accuracy, i.e.\ that leakage inflates scores---is already established across domains by prior work, including the general leakage-and-reproducibility analysis of \citet{kapoor2023leakage}, the long-range genomic regression study of \citet{rafi2025}, and the protein-interaction benchmarks of \citet{bushuiev2024}; we reproduce it here only as a within-suite control.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a per-dataset near-duplicate \emph{report card} for the binary suite with a matched clean-versus-leaky control, showing that leakage is dataset-specific rather than universal, and that a length-robust containment re-measure both confirms four clean verdicts and flags one borderline dataset the length-blind metric misses;
\item evidence that near-duplicate leakage can \emph{reorder the model ranking} when a memorization-prone model is in the comparison set---demonstrated on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, where accuracy, AUROC, $F_1$, a chromosome-holdout control and a bootstrap winner probability all agree, and a cautionary partial case on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters};
\item a mechanism, validated three ways: a graded memorization analysis plus a random-forest regularization path (the effect is a property of the \emph{default} unregularized forest), a from-scratch 1D CNN grid (the effect is not a classical-model artifact), and a chromosome-holdout and alignment-based re-measure (the effect is not a clustering or $k$-mer artifact);
\item a nine-model roster spanning $1$-nearest-neighbour to naive Bayes, showing the reordering is not an artifact of a four-model comparison: the leaky split ties its top three models within $0.0015$ while the corrected split spreads them over $23$ points, and the four models whose drops exclude zero are $1$-nearest-neighbour, the forest, extremely randomized trees and the multilayer perceptron; the remaining five---including $15$-nearest-neighbour, which we designated memorization-prone in advance---are null;
\item a \emph{cause}, identified in the shipped genomic coordinates and then manipulated: the leaky positive class is an un-deduplicated assembly shipping $77{,}421$ intervals on $40{,}934$ distinct coordinates, and editing that construction step alone abolishes the reordering in the leaky dataset and manufactures it in a clean one;
\item a cross-suite check on the Nucleotide Transformer downstream tasks, where three of eleven independent tasks are leaky---one by outright byte-identical duplication, the signature the coordinate defect produces though that release ships no coordinates to confirm the cause, two by cross-species splice-site homology---but no ranking inverts materially, a null whose reason is diagnosable in advance from the as-shipped split alone; and
\item a dependency-free, near-duplicate-aware splitter and an executable certification tool, with an honest statement of scope.
\end{itemize}
The classical audit is deterministic, CPU-only, and reproducible; the deep-model checks are pre-registered and run deterministically on a single device.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Leakage is an established benchmarking hazard. \citet{kapoor2023leakage} document leakage as a pervasive driver of the reproducibility crisis in ML-based science and show that it can inflate reported performance and, in extreme cases, change the apparent ordering of methods; our study is a genomics-specific, controlled instance of that phenomenon. In genomics, hashFrag \citep{rafi2025} shows that homologous sequences spanning genomic train/test splits inflate the apparent performance of neural models---test performance scales with similarity to the training set---and provides tools to detect and partition such data. In protein--protein interaction prediction, \citet{bushuiev2024} show that standard splits leave most test cases with near-duplicate counterparts, rewarding overfitting capacity rather than generalization; PINDER \citep{kovtun2024} similarly shows that de-leaked test sets sharply reduce reported performance, including for AlphaFold-Multimer. In virtual screening, an audit of LIT-PCBA \citep{huang2025} shows that a trivial memorization baseline can match sophisticated methods once leakage is accounted for.

The same failure mode is documented outside biology, and one strand of it asks our exact downstream question. In vision, \citet{barz2020cifar} find near-duplicates of training images inside the CIFAR test sets and show that purging them lowers reported accuracy, and \citet{northcutt2021labelerrors} show that test-set defects in ten widely used benchmarks are severe enough to \emph{destabilize the ranking} of the models evaluated on them. In natural language processing, \citet{elangovan2021leakage} attribute overstated performance to train/test overlap, and \citet{lee2022dedup} show that deduplicating training corpora changes what models learn. The problem is now acute for large language models, whose pretraining corpora absorb the benchmarks meant to evaluate them: \citet{sainz2023contamination} argue that contamination must be measured per benchmark before any result is believed, \citet{magar2022contamination} separate memorization from its exploitation at test time, \citet{deng2024contamination} detect contamination in current benchmarks directly, and \citet{zhou2023cheater} show under controlled injection that benchmark leakage inflates scores enough to distort model comparison. That literature is where the ranking question has been posed most sharply; what it lacks, and what a small curated suite supplies, is a matched clean control within the same collection.

Evaluation design is itself a recognized hazard in genomics. \citet{whalen2022pitfalls} catalogue the ways genomic machine-learning evaluations mislead---distribution shift, confounding, and inappropriate splits among them---and \citet{schreiber2020pitfall} show that a standard cross-cell-type setup can reward a model for exploiting a shortcut rather than learning the intended signal, while \citet{toneyan2022evaluating} find that conclusions about deep genomic models depend materially on evaluation choices. Near-duplicate leakage is a specific and measurable member of that family, and the one we can manipulate directly.

The closest methodological prior art is homology-aware partitioning, an idea with a long history: \citet{hobohm1992} introduced redundancy-reduced representative sets for protein data, and the practice is established enough that \citet{walsh2016correct} treat similarity-aware validation as a peer-review criterion rather than an optional refinement. GraphPart \citep{teufel2023graphpart} partitions biological sequences so that no train/test pair exceeds a similarity threshold, \citet{petti2022benchmark} cast the same problem as independent-set selection on a similarity graph, and CD-HIT \citep{li2006cdhit} and MMseqs2 \citep{steinegger2017mmseqs2} are the established clustering engines for this task. Our near-duplicate-aware splitter is a lightweight, dependency-free instance of the same whole-cluster idea; we validate against MMseqs2 alignment identity to confirm the effect is not an artifact of our $k$-mer edge metric.

We differ from prior genomics work in what we measure. Those studies document inflated absolute scores within a single leaky domain; we audit a whole suite with a within-study clean control and ask the downstream question---whether leakage reorders \emph{which model wins}. We also position our audit against the deep-model literature it is often compared to: DNABERT-2 \citep{zhou2023dnabert2}, HyenaDNA \citep{nguyen2023hyenadna}, and the Nucleotide Transformer \citep{dallatorre2024nucleotide} are pretrained on the same human reference genome from which these datasets are drawn, so evaluating them here would introduce a second, pretraining-based leakage channel that no train/test split can close (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); we therefore use a from-scratch CNN as the deep-model probe and scope the pretrained foundation models out of the split-effect evidence.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
We study the seven binary datasets of the Genomic Benchmarks suite \citep{gresova2023}, and additionally report the three-class \textit{human\_ensembl\_regulatory} set separately. Per-dataset sizes, sequence lengths, and class balance are given in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. Sizes range from 6{,}914 (\textit{drosophila\_enhancers\_stark}) to 174{,}756 (\textit{human\_ocr\_ensembl}) sequences; lengths are fixed within some datasets (e.g.\ 251\,bp for \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}) and variable in others; classes are balanced ($0.50$ positive fraction) except \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} ($0.544$). All balanced datasets are curated to $0.500$ in both train and test (positive/negative subfolders), so class balance is preprocessed, not natural---which is why we also run an imbalanced-prevalence stress test (\S\ref{sec:imbalance}).

\subsection{Features, models, and decision rule}
Each sequence is represented by overlapping $k$-mer counts for $k\in\{4,6\}$ ($4^k$ features). We evaluate four classifiers spanning a range of \emph{memorization propensity} (equivalently, effective regularization) rather than raw capacity: logistic regression (\texttt{C=1.0}, \texttt{max\_iter=5000}), a linear support-vector machine (\texttt{LinearSVC}, \texttt{C=1.0}, \texttt{dual=False}), a random forest (\texttt{150} trees, scikit-learn defaults \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf=1}, \texttt{max\_depth=None}), and gradient-boosted trees (\texttt{HistGradientBoosting}, library defaults). L2 normalization is applied \emph{only} to the two linear models, via a \texttt{Normalizer} pipeline step; the tree ensembles (random forest, HistGradientBoosting) consume raw counts, for which monotone feature scaling is irrelevant. We deliberately avoid a ``capacity'' framing: the nominally higher-capacity HistGradientBoosting is barely inflated while the random forest is heavily inflated, so the controlled variable is memorization propensity, not capacity. Unless noted, all accuracies use a single decision rule---\texttt{.predict()} (\texttt{argmax} of the class probabilities)---and the headline accuracy drop is the difference between the original accuracy and the mean corrected accuracy over five re-split seeds, with a percentile test-set bootstrap 95\% CI ($1{,}000$ resamples of the per-example correctness vector; models are not refit). To confirm strand-symmetry is not the driver, we also re-run the full comparison with canonical $k$-mer features, $\min(k\text{-mer},\text{reverse complement})$.

\subsection{Expanded nine-model roster}\label{sec:m-roster}
To test whether the ranking result depends on the size or composition of the comparison set, we widen it from four learners to nine, chosen to span memorization propensity end to end rather than to span accuracy. The four already described are reused \emph{verbatim}---same objects, same normalization, same seeds---and five are added: $1$- and $15$-nearest neighbours, extremely randomized trees ($150$ estimators, defaults), a multilayer perceptron (MLP; one hidden layer of $128$ units, early stopping, at most $60$ iterations), and Gaussian naive Bayes. We write MLP throughout and avoid the bare word ``perceptron'', which elsewhere denotes a linear model. Nearest neighbours is included because it is the \emph{definitional} memorizer---on a near-duplicate its prediction simply is the training label---so it bounds the axis from above; naive Bayes bounds it from below. Scale-sensitive learners (nearest neighbours, the MLP, and the two linear models) receive the same L2 normalization; tree ensembles are scale-invariant and Gaussian naive Bayes standardizes internally, so both take raw counts. All nine are trained from scratch on each split, so none carries the pretraining-overlap confound that scopes foundation models out of this study, and all use the same $k=6$ features and the same \texttt{.predict()} decision rule.

Two conventions matter for reading the result. First, an inversion criterion designed for four models is too permissive for nine: with a wider roster the top two are often within noise, and ``the top model changed by more than a point'' fires on a \emph{clean} control from repartitioning alone. We therefore additionally require that the two models exchanging the top slot each lead on their own split by a \emph{paired} cluster-bootstrap difference whose interval excludes zero. Second, we report the margin between the two models that swap and, separately, the margin between the as-shipped first and second place, because those are different quantities and the latter can be a statistical tie even when the former is large. Six predictions were committed in the modules before they were run, and every one is scored in the Results whether or not it held: \textbf{P1} $1$-nearest-neighbour shows the largest graded memorization gap; \textbf{P2} it shows the largest split-induced drop; \textbf{P3} the four memorization-prone learners outrank the two linear models on the as-shipped split and fall below them after correction; \textbf{P4} on a clean control no model's drop interval excludes zero. A sixth, \textbf{P6}, was committed in the dose-response module of \S\ref{sec:r-dose}: that a ranking inversion occurs at every imposed leak fraction exceeding $\varphi^{*}$ and at none below it. A fifth, \textbf{P5}, was committed in the tuning module of \S\ref{sec:m-tuning}: that cluster-grouped cross-validation would select a more regularized forest than naive cross-validation. Of the six, P1 fails as registered: on the raw gap it was committed to, the largest value belongs to the forest on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} and to $15$-nearest-neighbour on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}. We note, and label as post hoc, that under the prevalence-corrected gap of \S\ref{sec:m-graded}---a statistic we defined after P1 was committed, in response to the confound described there---$1$-NN does have the largest gap on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, though still not on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}; we do not count this as a confirmation. P2 holds on one leaky dataset and fails on the other, P3 and P4 hold, P5 holds on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} and ties on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}, and P6 holds on all ten doses.

\subsection{Near-duplicate measurement}\label{sec:m-measure}
We measure train/test overlap by the exact Jaccard similarity of $k$-mer sets ($k=8$), computed for all test--train pairs via sparse binary matrix products (no MinHash approximation). For each test sequence we record its maximum similarity to any training sequence, and define the leakage fraction as the proportion of test sequences whose maximum similarity exceeds a threshold ($0.5$, $0.7$, $0.9$). Because a $k$-mer-set Jaccard is bounded by the length ratio ($\text{Jaccard}\le L_{\min}/L_{\max}$), it undercounts leakage on datasets with variable or disparate lengths; we therefore also report a length-robust \emph{containment} index, $|A\cap B|/\min(|A|,|B|)$, in the same clustering, and disclose the length-pair cap per dataset (Table~\ref{tab:reportcard}). Jaccard leak fractions are thus lower bounds; the containment re-measure is the length-robust check on the clean verdicts. We additionally census exact byte-identical train/test duplicates: on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, $11{,}774$ test sequences ($0.380$ of the test set) are byte-identical training copies (leakage is literal duplication), whereas \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} has $0$ exact duplicates yet $0.225$ near-duplicates at Jaccard $\ge0.9$ (genuinely mutated copies)---the more homology-like but statistically weaker case.

\subsection{Near-duplicate-aware re-split}
To correct leakage we build a similarity graph in which sequences are connected when their $k$-mer Jaccard exceeds $0.7$, take connected components as near-duplicate clusters, and assign whole clusters to either train or test. The assignment preserves the original train/test ratio and class balance and, by construction, guarantees zero residual cross-split similarity above the threshold (verified empirically). We confirm robustness to the clustering threshold (sweeping $0.5/0.7/0.9$) and to removal of the single largest component. This is a dependency-free instance of the whole-cluster partitioning used by GraphPart \citep{teufel2023graphpart}, CD-HIT \citep{li2006cdhit}, and MMseqs2 \citep{steinegger2017mmseqs2}, any of which could substitute for the edge metric.

\subsection{Controls and auxiliary diagnostics}\label{sec:m-controls}
\paragraph{Alignment-based validation.}
To test whether the effect depends on the $k$-mer edge metric, we re-cluster the two leaky datasets with MMseqs2 \citep{steinegger2017mmseqs2} \texttt{easy-cluster} (\texttt{min-seq-id 0.7}, \texttt{-c 0.8}), feed the external cluster vector into the \emph{same} whole-cluster assignment path, and refit all four models---only the edge metric changes (alignment identity vs $8$-mer Jaccard), the linkage held fixed. Because MMseqs2 is itself $k$-mer-seeded, we describe this as an \emph{alignment-scored} cross-check, not a $k$-mer-independent one.

\paragraph{Leave-one-chromosome-out control.}
We recover source genomic coordinates for the two leaky datasets and build a chromosome-holdout split---whole chromosomes are assigned entirely to the test side until the target test fraction is reached (nine chromosomes for \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, twelve for \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters})---the field-standard control in regulatory genomics, refitting all four models. This control is deployment-relevant but \emph{not} leakage-free: residual near-duplicate leakage surviving the chromosome split is $0.8\%$ (\textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}) and $0.02\%$ (\textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}) at Jaccard $0.7$ (versus the exact $0$ guaranteed by the near-duplicate-aware split), which we report rather than hide.

\subsection{Regularization path and the graded memorization gap}\label{sec:m-graded}
To test whether the effect is an artifact of unregularized random-forest defaults, we sweep \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} $\in\{1,5,20,50,100,200,500\}$ and \texttt{max\_depth} $\in\{\text{None},16,8,4\}$ at full scale (the largest settings exceed the largest near-duplicate cluster, so no single leaf can be a pure duplicate block). The split-internal \emph{graded memorization gap} is $\mathrm{acc}[\ge0.9]-\mathrm{acc}[<0.5]$: accuracy on near-duplicate test sequences minus accuracy on novel ones. In its raw form this statistic is \textbf{not} confound-free, and we correct it. The two similarity strata have very different class composition, in opposite directions on the two leaky datasets---the $\ge0.9$ stratum is $99.97\%$ positive on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} against $19.68\%$ in the novel stratum, and $0.15\%$ against $98.17\%$ on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}. A constant classifier that always predicts the positive class, and therefore memorizes nothing, scores a raw gap of $+0.803$ on the first dataset and $-0.980$ on the second. Any model with a class-prediction bias inherits a large gap of the corresponding sign for free. We therefore report the gap computed from \emph{balanced} accuracy---the unweighted mean of per-class recall---within each stratum, for which a constant classifier scores $0.5$ in every stratum and hence a corrected gap of exactly zero, and we report the raw gap alongside it so the size of the confound is visible. A label-concordance analysis records whether each near-duplicate test sequence shares its nearest training neighbour's label.

\subsection{From-scratch 1D CNN grid}
As a deep-model probe we train a from-scratch 1D residual convolutional network in the Basset \citep{kelley2016basset} / DeepSEA \citep{zhou2015deepsea} lineage (one-hot input, no $k$-mer features) on each dataset's training split only, so there is no pretraining confound. We pre-specified, in a timestamped document included in the code release (\texttt{results/deep\_preregistration.md}), the narrowed claim, the dropout$\times$weight-decay grid, and an explicit refutation condition: if the standard-practice regularized cell (dropout $0.3$, weight decay $10^{-4}$) has a cluster-bootstrap homology-drop CI including $0$ on \emph{both} datasets, the strong ``any expressive learner is inflated'' reading is refuted and reported as a limitation. Models are deterministic (seed $0$), trained with early stopping on a held-out slice, on MPS (CPU fallback); the unregularized-to-convergence cell is a labelled manipulation check.

\subsection{Cluster (block) bootstrap}
Because near-duplicates violate the independence a sample-wise bootstrap assumes, we also resample whole within-test near-duplicate components (8-mer Jaccard $>0.7$ connected components) as blocks. We report whether any significance verdict changes, quantify the design effect via the one-way intraclass correlation (ICC), cross-check the block-bootstrap interval against an analytic Liang--Zeger cluster-robust standard error, and report a combined-source interval that folds the five re-split-seed variance into the test-resampling variance.

\paragraph{Imbalanced evaluation.}\label{sec:imbalance}
Because the balanced datasets are curated, we add a prevalence stress test (random forest, target positive fraction $\pi\in\{0.5,0.2,0.1\}$) scored with prevalence-aware metrics: AUPRC (against the prevalence baseline), MCC, balanced accuracy, AUROC, and minority-class precision/recall/$F_1$. The near-duplicate-aware splitter is extended with a per-class realized-fraction check so it preserves the target prevalence.

\paragraph{Tuning selection.}\label{sec:m-tuning}
To test whether standard model selection rescues a practitioner from the defect, we cross-validate \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf}$\in\{1,5,20,50,100,200,500\}$ for the random forest on the as-shipped \emph{training} set, under two schemes: ordinary stratified $5$-fold, and $5$-fold in which whole near-duplicate clusters (the same components used for the corrected split) are held out together. The first is what a practitioner would actually run; the second is the honest version of it. We report which leaf size each selects and what that selection then scores on both splits.

\paragraph{Injected-leakage multiclass construction.}
The only multiclass set (\textit{human\_ensembl\_regulatory}) is clean, so no naturally leaky multiclass data exist in the suite; we answer the multiclass question by construction, injecting label-carrying near-duplicate train$\to$test copies at fraction $f\in\{0,0.1,0.2,0.4\}$ (on a $20$k subsample; the mechanism is scale-independent) and comparing the random forest against logistic regression before and after the near-duplicate-aware split.

\paragraph{Definitions used in the robustness results.}\label{sec:m-diag}
Four quantities appear in the robustness results and are defined here. \emph{Cluster cohesion} is the edge density of a single-linkage near-duplicate component: the fraction of its $\binom{m}{2}$ member pairs that actually exceed the similarity threshold. A component built from genuine mutual near-duplicates has cohesion near $1$; one assembled by transitive chaining, where members are linked only through intermediates, has cohesion near $0$, and de-duplicating it removes sequences that are not in fact near-duplicates of one another. The \emph{design effect} is $1+(\bar m-1)\rho$ for mean block size $\bar m$ and intraclass correlation $\rho$, i.e.\ the factor by which correlated blocks inflate the variance of a mean relative to independent sampling; it is what makes a cluster bootstrap matter, or not. A \emph{novel-only} evaluation scores the model trained on the as-shipped split using only test sequences below $0.5$ similarity to training, which isolates generalization without retraining or re-splitting. Finally, the \emph{GC-in-distribution} restriction and the low-complexity check use \texttt{dustmasker} \citep{morgulis2006dust} to report the masked fraction of each sequence, distinguishing genuine locus duplication from repeat- or microsatellite-driven similarity.

\subsection{Coordinate-space construction signatures}\label{sec:m-coord}
Genomic Benchmarks distributes per-sequence intervals (\texttt{id,region,start,end,strand}) separately from the sequences its loader returns. We recover them offline and realign them to loader row order, which is deterministic (split, then sorted class directory, then sorted filename, the filename stem being the interval \texttt{id}). Recovered interval width equals sequence length for $100\%$ of rows in all eight datasets; that check is informative only for the four variable-length datasets, since it is satisfied trivially where every sequence has the same length. Because these coordinates are causally upstream of, and statistically independent from, the $k$-mer clustering that measures leakage, they provide a cross-modal test rather than a restatement. Per class we compute self-overlap redundancy $R_{\mathrm{self}}=1-n_{\mathrm{merged}}/n_{\mathrm{raw}}$ and the share of \emph{test} intervals overlapping any \emph{train} interval at $\ge t$ reciprocal overlap, $t\in\{{\ge}1\,\mathrm{bp},0.5,0.9,1.0\}$. Overlap is strand-agnostic, since strand is unrecorded for two datasets. The screen thresholds the maximum over classes of the $\ge50\%$ reciprocal cross-split statistic at $0.1$---the same cut already used for the sequence-space verdict---so it introduces no fitted parameter.

\subsection{Construct-and-break manipulations}\label{sec:m-manip}
To test the construction account causally rather than correlationally we intervene on the construction step alone. \emph{Break-a-clean}: \textit{human\_ocr\_ensembl}, drawn from the merged Ensembl Regulatory Build and clean by every measure, is rebuilt the way an un-deduplicated assembly is, by duplicating positives until the same $94.3\%$ share of positive rows sits on a multiplicity-2 coordinate as we measure on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} (which requires duplicating $89.1\%$ of them, since duplicating a fraction $r$ leaves $2r/(1+r)$ of rows on a duplicated coordinate), then downsampling to the control's exact size and class balance. \emph{Fix-a-leaky}: \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} receives the merge step its curators omitted---every duplicated coordinate collapsed to one representative row---with sequences, labels, model code and split ratio unchanged and negatives subsampled to restore the control's balance. Each manipulation is paired with an unmanipulated control at the same split seed. Only the positive class carries duplicated coordinates, so both interventions perturb balance and size unless corrected; we therefore report balance-matched arms as primary and disclose that the break-a-clean pair is matched on size as well while the fix-a-leaky pair is not, since size there cannot be equalised without discarding the rows the intervention is defined to remove.

\subsection{Cross-suite census and re-ranking}\label{sec:m-census}
We apply the census of \S\ref{sec:m-measure} unchanged to the Nucleotide Transformer downstream tasks \citep{dallatorre2024nucleotide}, then run the full audit pipeline---as-shipped split, near-duplicate-aware re-split, same four models, same decision rule---on the tasks it flags, with a clean task as control. One deviation must be declared, because it affects the task that anchors the cross-suite result. The \textit{enhancers} task ships only $400$ test sequences, too few to carry a usable interval, so for that task alone we pool train and test and draw a fresh stratified $80/20$ split before running both arms---not at the shipped ratio, since \textit{enhancers} ships only $2.6\%$ of its rows as test and redrawing at that ratio would leave the interval as unusable as before. Pooling redistributes the duplicate pairs, so the leak fraction we measure on the re-split ($\varphi=0.099$) is lower than the $25\%$ byte-identical figure the as-shipped split carries; the census number describes the release as shipped, the ranking number describes the split we actually trained on, and they are not the same quantity. The census is run at full scale: no training set is subsampled, and \texttt{crosssuite\_census.csv} records $n_{\text{train}}$ used equal to $n_{\text{train}}$ available on every task. An earlier pass capped training sets at $20{,}000$ and is reported in \S\ref{sec:r-census} as an error we corrected, because capping removes potential near-duplicate partners and three verdicts moved when it was lifted. Reported fractions remain lower bounds for the separate, length-dependent reason of \S\ref{sec:m-measure}. Two counting conventions matter. \textit{enhancers} and \textit{enhancers\_types} share identical sequence sets and differ only in label granularity, so they are one task scored twice; and \textit{promoter\_all} is the exact union of \textit{promoter\_no\_tata} and \textit{promoter\_tata} on both splits, with empty symmetric difference, so it is not independent of them either. Collapsing both nestings leaves eleven independent tasks of the thirteen shipped, and we report independent-task counts throughout. The three splice-site tasks are \emph{not} nested---\textit{splice\_sites\_all} is not the union of the acceptor and donor sets, and those two overlap in $35$ sequences of roughly $20{,}000$---so they count separately. And although the suite ships original and revised releases, we draw no before-and-after inference from the pair: they are not the same data recurated---the original splice tasks are keyed by UniProt accession across species while the revised are human genomic windows keyed by coordinate, and sequence length differs on eight of thirteen tasks---so each release is assessed on its own terms.

\subsection{A diagnostic condition for when leakage reorders}\label{sec:m-law}
Stratify a leaky test set by maximum similarity to training into a leaked stratum (similarity $\ge0.9$, fraction $\varphi$) and a novel stratum ($<0.5$), and write $h_M$ and $n_M$ for a model's accuracy in each and $g_M=h_M-n_M$ for the graded gap of \S\ref{sec:m-graded}. Then
\begin{equation}a_M\;\approx\;n_M+\varphi\,g_M,\label{eq:twostratum}\end{equation}
and for an ordered pair $(A,B)$ with $A$ leading the as-shipped split, writing $\delta=n_B-n_A$ and $\Delta g=g_A-g_B$, the as-shipped margin is $\varphi\Delta g-\delta$, so the benchmark reports the wrong winner when
\begin{equation}0<\delta<\varphi\,\Delta g,\qquad\text{equivalently }\varphi>\varphi^{*}=\delta/\Delta g.\label{eq:phistar}\end{equation}

Two honest qualifications, because \eqref{eq:twostratum} is easy to overstate. First, it is an \emph{approximation and not an identity}: the two strata do not partition the test set, since sequences in the intermediate $[0.5,0.9)$ band belong to neither. That band is negligible on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} ($1.4\%$ of the test set, reconstruction error $0.002$) but not elsewhere---$22.4\%$ on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} (error up to $0.031$) and about a third on the Nucleotide Transformer splice tasks, where the approximation error exceeds the margins it would be used to adjudicate. We therefore apply \eqref{eq:phistar} only where the intermediate band is small, and report the band's size wherever we use it. Second, \eqref{eq:phistar} is bookkeeping rather than theory---two strata and an ordering---so we report it as a diagnostic screen, valuable only because $\varphi$, $n$ and $g$ are all measurable on the as-shipped split, letting a curator evaluate the condition \emph{without building a corrected split}. It is tested out of sample in \S\ref{sec:r-census} and is not offered as a contribution in its own right.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
All classical experiments are deterministic with logged seeds, CPU-only, and free of external services; the deep-model runs are seeded and single-device. The near-duplicate-aware splitter is released as a standalone tool (\texttt{homology\_split.py}, dependencies \texttt{numpy}/\texttt{scipy}). Reported 95\% confidence intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals; the resampling unit is the individual test sequence (sample bootstrap) or the within-test near-duplicate component (cluster bootstrap), models are not refit, and seeds are fixed.

\paragraph{Software environment.} Results were produced with Python $3.13.3$, scikit-learn $1.8.0$, NumPy $2.4.6$, SciPy $1.17.1$, pandas $3.0.3$ and matplotlib $3.10.9$, with the datasets fetched through \texttt{genomic\_benchmarks} $1.0.0$; the from-scratch CNN used PyTorch $2.13.0$. Two external tools are called: MMseqs2 \texttt{18-8cc5c} for the alignment-scored re-clustering and \texttt{dustmasker} $1.0.0$ (BLAST+ $2.17.0$) for the low-complexity check. Model results are hardware-independent, being CPU-only and deterministic; reported runtimes, and the CNN runs (MPS backend, CPU fallback), are from an Apple M4 under macOS $26.4$. A complete pinned dependency list ships as \texttt{results/requirements.txt} in the code release. Note that \texttt{genomic\_benchmarks} $1.0.0$ re-extracts a dataset on every \texttt{download\_dataset} call, so we materialise a local cache once and read from it thereafter; audits run against a different release of the suite should re-derive the report card rather than assume ours transfers.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Leakage is dataset-specific and clean verdicts survive a length-robust re-measure}
Of the seven binary datasets, only two---\textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} and \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}---exceed a $10\%$ near-duplicate test fraction at Jaccard $0.7$ ($0.384$ and $0.406$ respectively); the remaining five are near zero, and the three-class \textit{human\_ensembl\_regulatory} set is likewise clean ($0.005$; Table~\ref{tab:reportcard}). Because a $k$-mer Jaccard is length-capped, we re-measured at full scale with the length-robust containment index. Containment reveals substantially more leakage on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} ($0.384\to0.845$; its $2$\,bp minimum length caps the length-pair Jaccard at $\approx0.004$), and it flags one dataset the length-blind metric misses: \textit{demo\_coding\_vs\_intergenomic\_seqs}, clean under Jaccard ($0.078$), crosses the threshold under full-scale containment ($0.131$), so we relabel it \emph{borderline}. The remaining four clean datasets stay well below the threshold under both metrics (largest full-scale containment $0.068$, \textit{human\_ocr\_ensembl}). Two datasets are thus clearly leaky, one is borderline (containment-only), and four are clean under both metrics; the containment result is itself a demonstration that a length-blind similarity can under-report leakage on variable-length data, so both metrics should be reported.

\subsection{Correcting the split lowers accuracy only on leaky datasets}
Under the near-duplicate-aware re-split, the best model's accuracy drops sharply on the leaky datasets---by $16.5$ points for the random forest on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} ($0.8596\to0.6951\pm0.0019$ over five re-split seeds; the seed-$0$ partition used for the intervals and for Table~\ref{tab:roster} gives $0.6957$, a $16.4$-point drop; combined-source 95\% CI $[15.4,17.4]$, folding the test-set and five-seed re-split variance) and $11.8$ points on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} ($0.9284\to0.8101\pm0.0055$ over five re-split seeds). For that second dataset the point estimate and the interval are computed on different estimands and we state both rather than pairing them misleadingly: the five-seed mean gives the $11.8$-point figure, while the combined-source interval---which additionally folds in the five re-split-seed variance, exactly as the \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} figure above does---is computed on the seed-$0$ partition, where the delta is $10.8$ points, giving 95\% CI $[8.8,12.9]$ (the cluster bootstrap alone gives $[9.2,12.6]$)---whereas every clean-dataset delta is null. All five non-leaky deltas include zero for the memorizing random forest as well as the linear models (the largest, \textit{drosophila\_enhancers\_stark} RF, is $+0.024$ with 95\% CI $[-0.008,0.058]$), so the positive control holds for the model that carries the effect. 

The negative control confirms causation: a random re-split of the same data, at matched size and balance, leaves the leaky-dataset accuracies essentially unchanged (best-model deltas $-0.002$ and $-0.001$, both CIs including zero). Only removing near-duplicates, not re-splitting per se, lowers the score (Fig.~\ref{fig:controls}). The corrected accuracy is stable across five re-split seeds and, independently, across five random-forest training seeds ($0.698\pm0.002$ on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, $0.821\pm0.003$ on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}), with the random forest demoted on every seed---so the effect is neither a fortunate partition nor model stochasticity.

\subsection{Near-duplicate leakage reorders the model ranking on one dataset}\label{sec:r-rank}
The consequence for method comparison is the central result, and it separates the two leaky datasets. On \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} the best model \emph{changes}: the default random forest, ranked first on the standard split (accuracy $0.860$), falls to last after correction (accuracy $0.696$), and \textit{LinearSVC} becomes best ($0.798$; corrected order LinearSVC$>$LR$>$HGB$>$RF; Fig.~\ref{fig:ranking}). 

This change holds along every axis we tested. (i)~It holds under all three metrics---the random forest is demoted to rank~4 under accuracy, AUROC ($0.805$ vs LinearSVC $0.870$ corrected), and $F_1$ ($0.635$ vs $0.796$)---so it is not a threshold artifact. (ii)~The bootstrap probability that the random forest ranks first goes from $P=1.0$ on the standard split to $P=0.0$ after correction, with \textit{LinearSVC} at $P=1.0$ (both sample- and cluster-bootstrap). (iii)~It reproduces under a chromosome-holdout control (\S\ref{sec:chrom}). 

The size of the forest's fall repays a closer look, because it decomposes into two distinct effects and only one of them is the split. Evaluated on the as-shipped split's \emph{novel} stratum---no re-splitting, no retraining---the forest scores $0.770$ against \textit{LinearSVC}'s $0.782$: the ranking has already changed, but by only $1.2$ points. After re-splitting and refitting it sits $10$ points below. The difference is a \emph{retraining penalty}: de-leaking removes near-duplicate partners from the training set as well as the test set, and the memorizer loses more from that than the other models do (writing $a^{\mathrm{corr}}$ for accuracy on the corrected split and $n$ for accuracy on the as-shipped split's novel stratum, the residual $a^{\mathrm{corr}}-n$ is $-0.074$ for the forest against $+0.012$ to $+0.016$ for the other three). Both components are real and both are consequences of the leakage, but they are different quantities: the first says the benchmark was ranking the wrong model, the second says the memorizer's advantage depended on training data it should not have had.

On \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} the picture is weaker. Under accuracy at threshold $0.5$ the random forest is demoted from rank~1 to rank~3 (corrected LR $0.829>$ HGB $0.827>$ RF $0.820>$ LinearSVC $0.808$; these four accuracies are the single seed-$0$ partition, used for an apples-to-apples within-split comparison, and the forest's $0.820$ here is the seed-$0$ value of the same quantity whose five-seed mean, $0.810$, gives the headline drop), but this is a statistical dead heat---the corrected accuracy CIs of the leaders overlap (RF $[0.812,0.828]$ vs LR $[0.821,0.837]$)---and it does not survive a threshold-free metric: under both AUROC and $F_1$ the random forest remains rank~1 (corrected AUROC $0.929$, the highest of the four). The bootstrap winner probability agrees: after correction no model dominates (LR $0.63$, HGB $0.31$, RF $0.06$, cluster-bootstrap). We therefore claim the ranking-change result cleanly for \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, as an existence proof, and only partially for \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}. Per-panel rank correlations (Fig.~\ref{fig:ranking}) are consistent but, with only four models, Kendall $\tau$ is too coarse to be a primary statistic---a fall from first to last flips only its own pairs, leaving $\tau\approx0$ despite being the most consequential change in the study.

\subsection{The reordering is not an artifact of a four-model comparison}\label{sec:r-roster}
Widening the comparison set from four learners to nine strengthens the reordering and removes the ``sklearn tree defaults'' reading entirely. The nine span the memorization axis end to end---$1$- and $15$-nearest neighbours, a random forest, extremely randomized trees, a multilayer perceptron (MLP), gradient boosting, a linear SVM, logistic regression, and Gaussian naive Bayes---all trained from scratch, on the same splits, with the same decision rule (Table~\ref{tab:roster}); predictions P1--P4 (\S\ref{sec:m-roster}) were committed before the run. The size and composition of the comparison set is the obvious objection to any claim about ``rankings'', and this roster is the direct answer to it.

On \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} the corrected winner is the linear SVM, the same model the four-model comparison selects, so the two analyses agree. What the wider roster adds is a sharper statement of the damage. \textbf{The leaky benchmark cannot separate its top three at all}: the MLP ($0.8736$), $1$-nearest-neighbour ($0.8729$) and extremely randomized trees ($0.8721$) finish within $0.0015$ of one another, and the paired interval on the top-two margin, $[-0.005,0.006]$, includes zero---a three-way tie. \textbf{On the corrected split those same three models span $23$ accuracy points} ($0.768$, $0.537$, $0.628$). The benchmark is not merely crowning the wrong model; it is blind to enormous real differences between models it ranks as equals.

The reordering itself is decisive and supported. The linear SVM rises from rank~5 to rank~1 and logistic regression from~6 to~2, while the MLP falls from~1 to~3, extremely randomized trees from~3 to~7, and \textbf{$1$-nearest-neighbour from rank~2 to rank~9, last}. The two models that exchange the top slot each lead on their own split by a margin whose paired cluster-bootstrap interval excludes zero ($[0.071,0.080]$ as shipped, $[0.024,0.035]$ after correction). The split between models is the one the memorization account predicts: \textbf{the four models whose drops exclude zero are $1$-NN, the forest, extremely randomized trees and the MLP; the remaining five---including both linear models, boosting and naive Bayes---are all null}. The MLP's presence in that group is worth stating plainly rather than filing it under ``memorizers'': it is not one of the four learners we designated memorization-prone in advance, and its inclusion says the inflation reaches a neural learner too. The clearest single number is $1$-NN, which scores $0.873$ on the as-shipped split and $0.537$---indistinguishable from chance---once its near-duplicate partners are removed.

The convolutional network is ranked fifth by the leaky split while its corrected accuracy is comparable to or better than that of the models ranked above it. Its pre-registered reference cell, built on the same corrected split by the same construction, scores $0.8272$ as shipped---behind the MLP, $1$-NN, extremely randomized trees and the forest---and $0.8131$ once corrected, which taken on its own would place it first. We report that with the caution its evidence deserves rather than the emphasis its conclusion would invite, because that single cell is the \emph{maximum} of the nine-cell regularization grid, whose corrected accuracies span $0.749$--$0.813$, and the linear SVM's corrected $0.7975$ falls \emph{inside} that range: five of the nine cells exceed it and four fall below. We therefore do not claim the network would win the corrected ranking, and we would be violating both our own inversion criterion (\S\ref{sec:m-roster}) and our own instruction not to read conclusions from individual grid cells (\S\ref{sec:r-deep}) if we did. What the comparison supports is weaker and still worth stating: the network is ranked fifth by the leaky split while its corrected accuracy is comparable to or better than that of models ranked above it, so the leaky split is at least not \emph{favouring} the architecture family this suite publishes baselines for---and a properly powered version of this comparison, with seed replication and an interval, is the single most useful experiment a follow-up could run.

Of P1 and P2 on this dataset, the second holds outright---$1$-NN has the largest drop ($0.336$)---and the first depends on which form of the gap is used, which is itself informative. Under the \emph{raw} gap it fails, the forest edging $1$-NN $0.230$ to $0.208$; under the prevalence-corrected gap it holds decisively, $1$-NN leading at $+0.461$ against the forest's $+0.319$ (\S\ref{sec:r-mech}). We record both rather than quoting whichever is convenient: the prediction was made about memorization propensity, and the corrected statistic is the one that measures it. On \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} \emph{both} fail---$15$-NN has the largest gap and extremely randomized trees the largest drop---and we report that rather than the ensembl outcome alone, since a pre-registration reported selectively is worth nothing. P3, that memorizers outrank the linear models before correction and fall below them after, holds decisively on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}: mean rank $4.50$ against $5.50$ as shipped, $7.25$ against $1.50$ after. P4 holds: on the clean control no model's drop interval excludes zero, for any of the nine.

On \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} the roster produces an even larger scramble---extremely randomized trees fall from rank~1 to~8 and $1$-NN rises from~3 to~1, with supported margins---but we decline to read it as strengthening that dataset's case. The reason is the prevalence-corrected graded gap of \S\ref{sec:r-mech}: on that dataset it does not separate the model families at all (memorizers $+0.122$ against $+0.179$ for the other five, the largest of all belonging to the MLP), so the movement is not attributable to near-duplicate memorization even though it is large. That agrees with the independent evidence that this dataset's clusters chain heavily and its corrected split over-removes genuine promoter signal. We note explicitly that a tempting alternative discriminator does \emph{not} work: the residual of each drop against $\varphi g$ is in fact \emph{larger} on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} (up to $+0.259$) than on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} (up to $+0.152$), so it cannot be used to prefer one dataset over the other---it measures the train-side retraining penalty of \S\ref{sec:r-rank}, which is a consequence of leakage on both. The wider roster therefore sharpens the existing verdict rather than overturning it: \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} remains the clean case and \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} the cautionary one.

\subsection{The mechanism is memorization of label-carrying near-duplicates}\label{sec:r-mech}
Binning test sequences by similarity to training reveals the mechanism directly (Fig.~\ref{fig:graded}). On \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, the default random forest scores $1.000$ on near-duplicate test sequences ($\ge0.9$ similarity) but only $0.770$ on novel ones ($<0.5$)---a graded gap of $+0.230$, versus $\approx0.035$ for the other models---and on the novel sequences the random forest ($0.770$) is actually \emph{worse} than the linear SVM ($0.782$). 

Correcting that raw comparison for its class-composition confound (\S\ref{sec:m-graded}) \emph{strengthens} the mechanism rather than weakening it. Recomputing every gap from balanced accuracy within each stratum---so that a constant classifier scores exactly zero---orders the nine-model roster on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} as the memorization account predicts and the raw statistic did not: $1$-nearest-neighbour, the definitional memorizer, now has by far the largest corrected gap ($+0.461$), followed by extremely randomized trees ($+0.370$) and the forest ($+0.319$). The bottom of the ordering is occupied by the two linear models ($+0.154$ and $+0.144$), naive Bayes ($+0.129$) and, lowest of all, $15$-nearest-neighbour ($+0.016$)---which is what the account predicts, since averaging over fifteen neighbours is precisely what stops a nearest-neighbour rule from memorizing. The four memorization-prone learners ($1$- and $15$-nearest neighbours, the forest and extremely randomized trees) average $+0.292$ against $+0.165$ for the remaining five, a separation the raw statistic does not produce. The correction also disposes of two values the raw statistic produced that were never interpretable as memorization at all: $15$-nearest-neighbour's raw $-0.684$ and naive Bayes's $-0.234$ become $+0.016$ and $+0.129$, i.e.\ artifacts of predicting the minority class in a stratum that is $99.97\%$ positive.

The same correction is equally informative where it removes a signal. On \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} the corrected gaps do \emph{not} separate the families---memorizers average $+0.122$ against $+0.179$ for the other five, the largest of all belonging to the MLP---so on that dataset the mechanism is not supported once class composition is accounted for. This is an independent statistic reaching the same clean-versus-cautionary split that \S4.3, the chromosome control and the cohesion analysis reach separately, and we take the convergence as the reason to scope the mechanism claim to \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}.

Memorization pays because near-duplicates carry the label: at $\ge0.9$ similarity, $99.99\%$ of near-duplicate test sequences share their nearest training neighbour's label on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} ($n=11{,}774$) and $99.95\%$ on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} ($n=2{,}037$), so recognizing a near-copy is correct essentially for free. On \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} the inflation is confined to the memorizing model: the random-forest drop is significant while the logistic-regression drop is null ($+0.0004$, 95\% CI $[-0.006,+0.007]$). Clean datasets have near-empty high-similarity bins, leaving nothing to memorize.

\subsection{The cause is a curation defect visible in the coordinates}\label{sec:r-coord}
The suite ships genomic intervals alongside its sequences, and they identify the defect directly. Of the $77{,}421$ positive intervals in \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, only $40{,}934$ are distinct: $36{,}487$ coordinates are shipped exactly twice and $4{,}447$ once, and every one of the $36{,}487$ duplicated pairs carries byte-identical sequences and a single label, so the coordinate redundancy \emph{is} the sequence redundancy. Because the shipped split is random, $75.5\%$ of test positives have their exact coordinate present in training.

A comparison internal to Ensembl ties the defect to a missing deduplication step. \textit{human\_ocr\_ensembl} and \textit{human\_ensembl\_regulatory}, drawn from the Ensembl Regulatory Build's merged ``MultiCell'' segmentation \citep{zerbino2015regbuild}, sit at $0.000$--$0.010$ self-overlap, whereas the un-deduplicated \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, whose positive class derives from the FANTOM5 CAGE enhancer atlas \citep{andersson2014fantom5}, sits at $0.471$. That contrast establishes an association, not a cause. Provider, organism and assembly are held fixed to the extent that all three are human regulatory element sets distributed through Ensembl, but the upstream resources differ, so this is \emph{not} one pipeline run with and without a merge step and we do not claim it is; the causal claim rests on the manipulations of \S\ref{sec:r-manip}, where the construction step is varied directly and everything else is held fixed by construction. Thresholding the maximum-over-classes cross-split coordinate overlap (\S\ref{sec:m-coord}) at the same $0.1$ cut used in sequence space separates the suite $7/8$, with the two leaky datasets at $0.755$ and $0.992$ against $0.073$ and below for the rest. The single miss is \textit{demo\_coding\_vs\_intergenomic\_seqs}, containment-borderline in sequence space but coordinate-clean, whose leakage is coding-paralog homology with no coordinate signature by construction---and whose \texttt{coding\_seqs} class is keyed by transcript accession, one region per row, so its coordinate statistics are structurally zero rather than measured. Coordinates may therefore escalate a verdict but must never clear one. Two further refinements were forced by the data: redundancy is not always in the positive class (\textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} carries essentially all of its own in the \emph{negative} class, $R_{\mathrm{self}}=0.961$ against $0.047$), and mere adjacency over-calls (\textit{drosophila\_enhancers\_stark} tiles at $2142$\,bp with base-pair-scale touches, $0.406$ at ${\ge}1$\,bp but $0.036$ at $\ge50\%$ reciprocal overlap).

\subsection{Editing the construction step alone creates and cures the reordering}\label{sec:r-manip}
Editing the construction step alone abolishes the reordering in a leaky dataset and manufactures it in a clean one, which turns the correlational coordinate evidence of \S\ref{sec:r-coord} into a causal claim in both directions (\S\ref{sec:m-manip}, Table~\ref{tab:manip}). Applying the omitted merge step to \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, at matched class balance, collapses its leak fraction from $0.390$ to $0.012$ and the random-forest inflation from $+0.165$ to $-0.008$---a null---and the reordering disappears, the forest sitting at rank~4 both before and after correction. Conversely, imposing the same defect on the merged, clean \textit{human\_ocr\_ensembl}, at identical size \emph{and} balance, raises its leak fraction from $0.004$ to $0.204$ and its random-forest inflation from $+0.002$ to $+0.105$, and manufactures the reordering: the forest is promoted from rank~4 to rank~1 on the contaminated split and demoted to rank~4 again by correction---the syndrome of \S4.3, produced on demand. The break-a-clean effect is larger under matching than without it ($+0.063\to+0.105$), so the balance and size confounds were suppressing rather than creating it.

Three caveats belong with this result. Duplicated rows scattered across a random split are leaky by construction, so the break-a-clean direction is unsurprising in itself; its content is that the full downstream syndrome, including the memorizer's promotion to rank~1, follows from the construction step alone. The size-matching downsample removes some of the duplicate partners it has just introduced, so the imposed leak fraction ($0.204$) is about half the undownsampled value ($0.498$), making the resulting effect conservative. Each arm carries a two-arm cluster-bootstrap interval over whole within-test near-duplicate components, and the two directions are cleanly separated: the leaky control's random-forest inflation is $+0.165$ $[0.157,0.173]$ while the merged, balance-matched version of the same dataset gives $-0.008$ $[-0.019,0.003]$, a null; imposing the defect on the clean dataset moves it from $+0.002$ $[-0.019,0.025]$ to $+0.105$ $[0.083,0.126]$. Control and intervention intervals are disjoint in both directions. Each condition remains a single fit at one split seed, so we still read the contrast between arms rather than small differences within one.

\subsection{The effect is a property of the default unregularized random forest}\label{sec:r-regpath}
Regularizing the random forest dissolves the entire phenomenon monotonically. At the default (\texttt{min\_samples\_leaf=1}), the forest scores exactly $1.000$ on the $\ge0.9$-similarity bin (pure leaves form around individual near-duplicates), with the large drop ($0.164$ on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, $0.108$ on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}), the large graded gap ($0.230/0.121$), and rank~1. Increasing \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf} to $500$ drives the homology drop to $\approx0$ ($0.164\to-0.001$; $0.108\to0.000$), collapses the graded gap ($0.230\to0.020$; $0.121\to0.061$), and lowers accuracy on the $\ge0.9$ bin from $1.000$ to $\sim0.71$--$0.82$; capping \texttt{max\_depth} drives the drop to $\approx0$ likewise. Crucially, the ``random forest wins on the leaky split'' phenomenon itself \emph{requires} the unregularized forest: on the standard split the forest holds rank~1 only near the default and has fallen to rank~4 by \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf}$\ge20$ on both datasets. Either intervention---heavy regularization or a near-duplicate-aware split---reveals the truth; the leakage inflation and demotion are a property of the default high-capacity forest practitioners deploy.

\subsection{Deep models confirm the mechanism}\label{sec:r-deep}
A from-scratch 1D CNN, trained only on each training split, shows the same inflation, so the effect is not a classical-model artifact. The pre-registered standard-practice regularized reference cell (dropout $0.3$, weight decay $10^{-4}$) drops on \emph{both} datasets---$+0.043$ on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} (cluster-bootstrap CI $[0.025,0.061]$) and $+0.014$ on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} ($[0.007,0.021]$), both excluding zero---so the pre-registered refutation condition is \emph{not} triggered. Nineteen of the $20$ cells drop with cluster-bootstrap CIs excluding zero (all nine \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} grid cells, eight of nine \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} grid cells, and both manipulation checks); the sole exception is one \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} cell (dropout $0.6$, weight decay $10^{-4}$) where heavy dropout under-fits, leaving no inflation to lose (accuracy rises $0.020$ after correction). Per-cell single-run variance is non-trivial, so we read the mechanism from the grid-level contrast---regularized reference versus unregularized manipulation check---rather than from individual cells. 

The unregularized-to-convergence manipulation-check cell memorizes hardest, mirroring the unregularized random forest: on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} its graded gap is $+0.222$ (accuracy $0.974$ on the $\ge0.9$ bin vs $0.752$ novel) with drop $+0.076$ $[0.068,0.084]$. One pre-registered expectation was not met, and we report it rather than quietly restate the grid: the pre-registration named this a \emph{dose-response} grid, and it is not one. Across the nine cells the drop is non-monotone in both dropout and weight decay (on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} it runs $0.021/0.036/0.058$, $0.018/0.014/0.064$, $0.029/0.063/0.016$), so the grid establishes that the inflation reaches a from-scratch neural network but not that it scales smoothly with regularization strength. The one clean contrast is training duration: the unregularized-to-convergence check differs from the matched grid cell only in epochs. This is consistent with ``memorization propensity, not capacity''.

\subsection{A second suite carries the defect; its rankings do not invert, as the condition predicts}\label{sec:r-census}
Applying the census unchanged to the Nucleotide Transformer downstream tasks shows the defect is not confined to Genomic Benchmarks. Counting independent tasks, \textbf{three of eleven} in the original release are leaky and \emph{six more borderline}, leaving only two clean. We report this at full scale: an earlier pass capped training sets at $20{,}000$ and returned three borderline and five clean, and re-running uncapped moved three histone tasks across the cut on the length-robust containment index---which is exactly the failure our own first practice recommendation warns against, and the same correction the GUE census required. The enhancer task carries $25.0\%$ test-to-train leakage that is \emph{byte-identical}---verified on a separate code path using no $k$-mers, $100$ of $400$ test sequences with label concordance $1.000$, in a training set holding $14{,}968$ rows on $14{,}002$ unique sequences---while \textit{splice\_sites\_acceptors} and \textit{\_donors} carry $0.244$ and $0.245$ at Jaccard $0.7$ by a different mechanism, with zero exact duplicates, both being multi-species sets whose near-duplicates are homologous splice sites across organisms.

We then ran the full pipeline there, and report a null: \textbf{no material ranking inversion} on any of the three leaky tasks, nor on the clean control. Leakage inflates scores as expected---on \textit{enhancers} the random forest drops $0.0413$ $[0.0241,0.0583]$, \textit{LinearSVC} $0.0221$ $[0.0055,0.0381]$ and HistGradientBoosting $0.0185$ $[0.0021,0.0366]$, all excluding zero---but the leading model is unchanged everywhere.

Condition~\eqref{eq:phistar} gets its first out-of-sample test here. Two of the twenty-four pairs are informative, and it was right on both---but only one of the two is admissible under our own rule, and we say so before anyone else does. \S\ref{sec:m-law} permits \eqref{eq:phistar} only where the intermediate $[0.5,0.9)$ band is small and requires the band be reported wherever it is applied. Measured here, that band is $0.0\%$ of the test set on \textit{enhancers} and $33.3\%$ on \textit{splice\_sites\_acceptors}---the regime \S\ref{sec:m-law} names as one where the approximation error exceeds the margins being adjudicated, and the acceptors pair's as-shipped margin is $0.0032$, the smaller of the two informative pairs. So the correct no-swap call for \textit{splice\_sites\_acceptors} RF-versus-HGB ($\varphi=0.077$ against $\varphi^{*}=0.257$) is \emph{inadmissible} and we do not count it. What remains is a single admissible informative call: a predicted swap for \textit{enhancers} RF-versus-HGB ($\varphi=0.099$ against $\varphi^{*}=0.073$); HistGradientBoosting does overtake the forest after correction ($0.887$ versus $0.869$), which is precisely the case a trivial baseline gets wrong. Every prediction was emitted from as-shipped measurements before any corrected split existed. Three limits keep this modest. The aggregate score is nearly uninformative and we do not lean on it: $24$ of $24$ pair orders were predicted correctly, but a constant ``nothing swaps'' predictor scores $23$ of $24$ on the same data, so all but two of the twenty-four opportunities are vacuous by construction. The swap the condition caught is itself \emph{immaterial} under our own convention, since the two models were separated by only $0.005$ on the as-shipped split---which is why the task reports no inversion. And one admissible informative call, correct, is real but slender evidence, which we do not present as more.

The reason for the null is the condition's own: $\delta\le0$ on twenty-two pairs, because on these tasks the model leading the as-shipped split is, with one exception, also the best model on novel sequences---logistic regression on \textit{enhancers} and on the control task, the forest on \textit{splice\_sites\_donors}---so there is nothing to invert. The exception is \textit{splice\_sites\_acceptors}, where boosting is best on novel sequences while the forest leads as shipped; there the condition correctly identified a possible inversion and correctly predicted it would not occur, $\varphi=0.077$ falling below $\varphi^{*}=0.257$. Reordering needs a challenger that is actually better on novel sequences \emph{and} a leak fraction above $\varphi^{*}$; this suite supplies the second condition without the first. The reordering result therefore remains a single-dataset existence proof, now externally stress-tested rather than merely asserted.

\paragraph{A second, pre-registered cross-suite test, which we failed.}
Our pre-registration also carried binding predictions for GUE \citep{zhou2023dnabert2}, recorded before any of that suite was examined: that its short fixed-length human regulatory tasks---core promoter at $70$\,bp, promoter at $300$\,bp, transcription-factor binding at $101$\,bp---would be leaky, and its multi-species tasks clean. Executed at full scale, the registered predictions score $3$ of $15$, and \textbf{all eleven predicted-leaky tasks are clean}, with near-duplicate fractions from $0.009$ to $0.041$ under the $k$-mer Jaccard and $0.014$ to $0.042$ under containment---the largest under half the verdict cut, on both metrics. We count these conservatively, because they are not eleven independent benchmarks. The \textit{\_all} promoter tasks' \emph{test sets} are the exact union of their own \textit{notata} and \textit{tata} test sets, with empty symmetric difference---the training sets are not, each family withholding a different ${\approx}10\%$ as an unshipped development partition; and the $70$\,bp and $300$\,bp promoter families are the same loci at two window widths---$90\%$ of the $70$\,bp test windows occur verbatim inside the $300$\,bp corpus, at the fixed offset $[216{:}286]$. The independent count is therefore \textbf{seven} test partitions: two promoter partitions and five transcription-factor tasks. This nesting is between tasks and does not affect any verdict, since the census measures test against train within a task, but it does bound how much independent evidence the null carries.

This refutes the hypothesis the prediction encoded, and we report it as a failure rather than omit it. Leakage does not track task type: seven independent benchmarks in precisely the regime we flagged carry none of it. What it tracks is the construction step of \S\ref{sec:r-coord}---Genomic Benchmarks' two leaky datasets have an un-deduplicated assembly where GUE's comparable tasks do not. The consequence for our own scope claim is stated in \S\ref{sec:discussion}: the regime is not \emph{sufficient} for leakage, and we no longer offer it as the reason to expect it.

One further verdict moved under the full-scale re-run and we record it: \textit{emp\_H3K4me3}, registered clean, has a containment leak fraction of $0.137$ at full scale against $0.093$ on a capped run, which makes it borderline rather than clean. Under the binary scoring rule committed in advance (leaky against not-leaky) it remains a correct prediction and the score stands at $3$ of $15$; under a three-way rule it would be a miss and the score would be $2$ of $15$. We keep the committed rule and report both figures rather than choose between them after the fact.

\subsection{A dose-response test: the condition predicts where the ranking flips}\label{sec:r-dose}
The breakdown condition \eqref{eq:phistar} and the construct-and-break manipulation have so far been argued separately---the first is bookkeeping over as-shipped quantities, the second a single before-and-after contrast. Together they license a sharper test. Because the manipulation \emph{sets} $\varphi$, and $\varphi^{*}$ is computed from quantities measured before any corrected split exists, the condition makes a quantitative prediction about the dose at which the ranking should flip. We registered that prediction (\textbf{P6}: inversion at every dose with $\varphi>\varphi^{*}$ and at none below) in the module before running it.

We imposed the duplicated-coordinate construction on \textit{human\_ocr\_ensembl}, which is clean, at ten doses, each downsampled to the control's size and class balance so that arms differ only in near-duplicate content. At each dose we measured $\varphi$, computed $\varphi^{*}$ for the as-shipped leader against every challenger using only as-shipped novel-stratum accuracies and graded gaps, and then asked whether the ranking actually inverts under the near-duplicate-aware re-split.

\textbf{The condition is right on all ten doses.} As the dose rises the random forest climbs the as-shipped ranking---rank~4 at doses up to $0.20$, rank~3 at $0.25$, rank~1 from $0.30$ on---while remaining last on the corrected split at \emph{every} dose, including the untouched control. The ranking first inverts at dose $0.30$, where $\varphi=0.106$ against a predicted breakdown point of $\varphi^{*}=0.060$, and inverts at all six higher doses; it does not invert at the three lower ones. The prediction is made from the as-shipped split alone, which is the property that makes the condition useful to a curator. As \S\ref{sec:m-law} requires wherever \eqref{eq:phistar} is applied, we report the intermediate $[0.5,0.9)$ band: it runs $1.2$--$1.5\%$ of the test set across all ten doses, so the two-stratum approximation is well inside the regime where we permit its use.

Two qualifications bound what this establishes, and together they matter enough that we state them before anyone else does.

First, the condition can predict ``no inversion'' two ways: because no challenger has both $\delta>0$ and $\Delta g>0$, so no inversion is possible at any leak fraction; or because such a pair exists but $\varphi$ has not reached $\varphi^{*}$. All three of our negatives are of the first kind. The manipulation moves $\varphi$ and the forest's leadership together---at the dose where it first leads, $\varphi$ already exceeds $\varphi^{*}$---so the two regimes are not separable here and the sub-threshold arm is not exercised.

Second, and more sharply: on these ten doses the rule ``the ranking inverts exactly when the forest leads the as-shipped split'' also scores $10/10$, using no novel-stratum accuracies, no graded gaps and no $\varphi^{*}$ at all. The dose-response therefore shows that the condition is \emph{consistent} with a causal manipulation of $\varphi$; it does \emph{not} show that the condition beats that much simpler rule, because this manipulation confounds the two. Separating them requires a case where a memorization-prone model leads the as-shipped split and the ranking nonetheless does not invert, which is the regime the Nucleotide Transformer suite occupies and where the condition earns its keep for a different reason---$\delta\le0$ on twenty-two pairs, so no leak fraction could have inverted them (\S\ref{sec:r-census}). We report the dose-response as causal support for the condition's form, not as evidence that it is the minimal correct predictor.

\subsection{Three controls: alignment identity, chromosome holdout, and block resampling}\label{sec:r-controls}
\paragraph{Alignment identity defuses the $k$-mer-circularity concern.}
Re-clustering by MMseqs2 alignment identity, feeding the same whole-cluster splitter, reproduces the \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} effect metric-independently: the random-forest drop is $0.164$ under $8$-mer Jaccard and $0.162$ under alignment identity, the other models stay near zero under both, and the random forest is still demoted to rank~4. On \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} the drop is milder under alignment ($0.108\to0.064$; corrected random-forest accuracy $0.82\to0.864$), i.e.\ partly $k$-mer-specific---reinforcing its partial status. Because MMseqs2 is $k$-mer-seeded we claim ``alignment-scored,'' not ``$k$-mer-independent''.

\paragraph{A chromosome-holdout control.}\label{sec:chrom}
Under the genomics-standard chromosome-holdout split, the marquee demotion reproduces on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}: the random forest falls from rank~1 ($0.8596$) to rank~4 ($0.7069$), giving the identical corrected order (LinearSVC$>$LR$>$HGB$>$RF) as the near-duplicate-aware split. On \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} it does \emph{not}: the random forest stays rank~1 ($0.9284\to0.8199$; order RF$>$HGB$>$LR$>$LinearSVC), a third independent signal (with the AUROC/$F_1$ reversal and the novel-only $\tau=-0.67$) that its demotion is the cautionary partial case. The two controls agree on the drop magnitude (both single-split, seed~0: chromosome-holdout corrected $0.707/0.820$ vs near-duplicate-aware $0.696/0.820$), validating the near-duplicate-aware split as a good proxy for the deployment control; residual near-duplicate leakage surviving the chromosome split is $0.8\%$ and $0.02\%$, so it is standard but not leakage-free.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:r-robust}
\paragraph{The cluster (block) bootstrap changes no verdict.}
Resampling whole within-test near-duplicate components as blocks (the correct tool for correlated near-duplicates) widens the CIs by $1.0$--$1.8\times$ but flips no significance verdict: the random-forest drop still excludes zero on both datasets (\textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} $[0.155,0.173]$; \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} $[0.092,0.126]$) and the \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} logistic-regression drop stays null. The design effect is small because the correlated near-duplicates are test-to-\emph{train}, not test-to-test---within-test clustering is only $1.06$--$1.19$ sequences per component---and a sample-wise bootstrap of a fixed trained model's test correctness is nearly unbiased in that regime. The intraclass correlation is high ($0.78$--$0.99$) yet the design effect is small ($1.06$--$1.17$); the analytic cluster-robust interval agrees with the block bootstrap, and a combined-source interval that additionally folds in the five re-split-seed variance still excludes zero for both leaky random-forest drops (\textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} $[0.154,0.174]$; \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} $[0.088,0.129]$), while every clean and three-class delta remains null. 

Because we report roughly a dozen leaky-versus-clean delta intervals, we also applied a Benjamini--Hochberg correction at $q=0.05$: it leaves the two leaky random-forest drops and the \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} logistic-regression drop significant and no clean or three-class delta significant, so multiplicity changes no verdict.

\paragraph{Metric geometry.}
The findings are robust to how similarity and features are defined. The length-robust containment index reveals more leakage on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} ($0.845$) and flags \textit{demo\_coding} as borderline ($0.131$), while the other four clean datasets stay below the threshold (\S4.1). Canonical (reverse-complement-collapsed) $k$-mer features keep the demotion---random forest $1\to4$, drop $0.153$ on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}; $1\to3$, drop $0.097$ on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}---so it is genuine memorization, not forward-strand overfitting; canonicalization barely changes the leakage fraction (\textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} $0.406\to0.416$). 

The drop is not a GC covariate-shift artifact: restricting the corrected test set to GC-in-distribution sequences gives \emph{lower} accuracy ($0.787$ nontata, $0.631$ ensembl), not the inflated original. 

Finally, single-linkage cohesion explains the ensembl-vs-nontata asymmetry: \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} clusters are $99.6\%$ pairwise with largest-cluster cohesion $0.96$ (clean discrete $\sim2\times$ locus duplication---correction is a clean fix), whereas \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}'s $420$-member largest cluster has cohesion $0.083$ ($24\%$ pairwise), i.e.\ heavy transitive chaining that over-removes genuine promoter/gene-family signal---the biological reason its demotion is partial. The leaked near-duplicates are not low-complexity or repeat-driven: their dustmasker-masked fraction ($0.033$ on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}, $0.053$ on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}) is low and comparable to that of novel sequences ($0.106$ and $0.043$ respectively)---below on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} and within a point on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}---so the near-duplication is genuine discrete locus duplication rather than a transposable-element or microsatellite artifact, and its removal is a legitimate correction.

\paragraph{Imbalanced setting.}
Under prevalence imbalance the leakage inflation is revealed by prevalence-aware metrics but masked by accuracy. On \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} at $\pi=0.2$, correcting the split lowers AUPRC $0.622\to0.477$, MCC $0.422\to0.182$, and minority recall $0.258\to0.070$, while accuracy barely moves ($0.844\to0.808$); \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} shows the same pattern. Accuracy at threshold $0.5$ is thus the wrong metric under imbalance, and the appropriate metrics show the inflation even more starkly. The prevalence-aware splitter preserves the target prevalence (realized positive fraction $0.5001/0.2007/0.1001\approx$ target), so it handles imbalance without skewing the minority class.

\paragraph{Injected-leakage multiclass.}
Injected near-duplicate contamination reproduces the mechanism in the multiclass setting. The random-forest drop grows monotonically with the injected fraction $f$ ($+0.0004$ at $f{=}0$; $+0.118$ at $0.1$; $+0.179$ at $0.2$; $+0.258$ at $0.4$), while logistic regression stays nearly flat ($\le0.066$), and the corrected accuracy is stable ($\sim0.58$) regardless of $f$ because the split removes the injected leakage. We inject rather than measure because the suite ships no naturally leaky multiclass set: its only multiclass set, the three-class \textit{human\_ensembl\_regulatory}, is clean. The mechanism therefore generalizes to the multiclass setting, appears only when $f>0$, scales with $f$, and hits only the memorizer by construction.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Reported performance on some popular genomic benchmarks overstates generalization and---more consequentially---can mislead about which method is best. Anyone using these suites to rank models risks selecting a method for its propensity to memorize near-duplicates rather than to generalize. The controlled variable behind this is memorization propensity (equivalently, effective regularization), not model capacity: the paper's own result---the nominally higher-capacity HistGradientBoosting is barely inflated while the default random forest is heavily inflated, and heavy regularization removes both the memorization and the apparent lead---contradicts a capacity account and supports a regularization one.

The sharpest objection available against our own result is that the reordering requires the \emph{unregularized} forest, which by \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf}$\ge20$ has already fallen to rank~4 on the as-shipped split (\S\ref{sec:r-regpath}); a reader may therefore ask whether we have shown only that leakage reorders untuned library defaults. \textbf{Tuning does not rescue the practitioner: cross-validation on the as-shipped training set selects the memorizing configuration.} The decisive answer is empirical rather than rhetorical, so we ran it. A practitioner does not know the split is leaky; they tune the way everyone tunes, by cross-validating on the training set. We therefore cross-validated the forest over \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf}$\in\{1,\dots,500\}$ on the as-shipped training set of both leaky datasets, once with ordinary stratified $5$-fold and once with folds that hold whole near-duplicate clusters out together---the leakage-aware version of the same procedure.

On \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, the dataset carrying the reordering claim, the two procedures diverge and the consequence is stark. Ordinary cross-validation selects \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf}$=1$, the memorizing default, and the model it selects scores $0.860$ on the as-shipped split but $0.696$ once the leakage is removed---a loss of $16.4$ points. Cluster-grouped cross-validation instead selects \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf}$=20$, and \emph{that} model loses only $2.2$ points ($0.751\to0.729$). So tuning does not rescue the practitioner: ordinary tuning actively selects the configuration whose apparent advantage is almost entirely leakage. Worse, the benchmark punishes the correct methodology---the leakage-aware tuner's model appears $10.8$ points \emph{worse} on the as-shipped split ($0.751$ against $0.860$) while in fact being $3.3$ points \emph{better} on the corrected one ($0.729$ against $0.696$), a point-estimate comparison to which we attach no interval. A practitioner who does the right thing is penalised by the benchmark for doing it.

On \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} both procedures select \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf}$=1$, so our pre-registered expectation P5---that the grouped procedure would select a more regularized forest---holds on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} and is a tie, rather than a reversal, on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}. The objection is answered on the dataset where it matters: the untuned default is not a careless choice a tuner would correct, it is \emph{what ordinary tuning selects}, and the configuration in which the reordering bites is the one a practitioner doing standard model selection would deploy.

Two further considerations point the same way. First, the relevant property of a benchmark is not whether some particular user tunes, but whether the benchmark \emph{rewards memorization at all}: a suite on which the leading model is leading because it memorizes is misreporting generalization regardless of who is reading it, and our regularization path shows the reward is real and monotone rather than an artifact of one setting. Second, and most directly, \S\ref{sec:r-manip} shows the defect is a property of the \emph{data} and not of the model: editing coordinates alone, with the model code untouched, both removes the reordering and creates it. Tuning is a mitigation a careful practitioner may apply; it is not a reason the benchmark is sound.

What we claim about model family is narrower than the tuning result, and we scope it explicitly: \textbf{when a memorization-prone learner is in the comparison set, this benchmark can report it as the winner for the wrong reason}. That condition is met whenever a tree ensemble at library defaults is compared against linear or well-regularized models---a common configuration, and the one in which our result bites---and it is not met by a CNN-only comparison. The related and, we think, fairer objection is therefore about model family rather than tuning, and here we concede more. The suite's own published baseline is a convolutional network \citep{gresova2023}, not a tree ensemble, and our from-scratch CNN is inflated far less than the forest---its regularized reference cell drops $0.014$ on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} against the forest's $0.164$. Whether a CNN-based leaderboard on this suite is affected is therefore \emph{open}, and we say so rather than resolving it in our favour: the network is ranked fifth by the leaky split while its corrected accuracy is comparable to models ranked above it (\S\ref{sec:r-roster}), which is suggestive of under-rating but rests on a nine-cell grid whose spread covers the comparison value. Settling it needs a replicated, interval-bearing CNN comparison that we have not run. The general lesson is about the benchmark's capacity to reward memorization, which \S\ref{sec:r-manip} shows is a property of its construction; which particular published rankings are affected depends on whose models are being compared, and we do not extrapolate beyond the comparison set we ran.

\textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} is an existence proof: the ranking change holds on every axis we tested (accuracy, AUROC, $F_1$, a bootstrap winner probability, a chromosome-holdout control, and an alignment-scored re-measure). \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} is a cautionary partial case: five independent signals (CI overlap, AUROC/$F_1$ reversal, chromosome control, novel-only $\tau=-0.67$, and a milder alignment drop) show its demotion is accuracy-only, and its single-linkage clusters chain heavily ($0.083$ cohesion), so aggressive de-duplication over-removes genuine promoter/gene-family signal and the random forest retains a real edge on novel sequences. The general reading is thus that near-duplicate leakage can reorder rankings \emph{when a memorization-prone model is in the comparison set}---demonstrated cleanly $n{=}1$ and partially on a second.

Because the effect is dataset-specific, the contribution is constructive: not ``these benchmarks are broken'' but ``here is which datasets to trust, and a tool to certify any new one.'' We package this as a per-dataset report card (Table~\ref{tab:reportcard}) and a dependency-free splitter. The certification tool additionally reports \emph{cluster cohesion}---the fraction of member pairs in the largest near-duplicate component that are genuine edges---because whole-cluster assignment is only a legitimate correction when components are near-duplicate sets rather than single-linkage chains. Below a cohesion of $0.5$ it returns \emph{leaky-but-correction-unsafe} rather than \emph{leaky}, and directs the user to the novel-stratum readout on the as-shipped split instead of a re-split. The cut is calibrated on the two leaky datasets studied here, which fall an order of magnitude either side of it: \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} has cohesion $0.96$ and \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} $0.08$. That is the quantitative form of the distinction we draw between the clean case and the cautionary one. We are careful with the report card's green verdicts, which carry a heavier evidential burden than red ones: a ``clean'' entry means only \emph{no forward-strand near-duplicate leakage detected} (subject to the disclosed length-pair cap), not certified homology-free. A practical caution concerns scale: subsampling can mask leakage (\textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} appears almost clean at $20$k sequences yet is heavily leaky at full scale), so audits must be run at full dataset scale. We paid that cost twice ourselves. The GUE census's first pass capped training sets at $20{,}000$ rows; re-run in full, the leak fractions rose by up to $2.8\times$ on the tasks large enough to have been capped---the seven below the cap are of course unchanged---and one verdict moved from clean to borderline, though the conclusion did not. The cross-suite census had the same defect and we missed it a round longer: re-running it uncapped moved three histone tasks across the cut and changed the reported tally from three leaky, three borderline and five clean to three, six and two (\S\ref{sec:r-census}).

\paragraph{What the detector detects, and one false positive.}
Two limits of the primary detector deserve statement, because both bear on verdicts we report. First, an $8$-mer Jaccard at threshold $0.7$ is not a fixed instrument across sequence length: a $70$\,bp sequence holds only $63$ $k$-mers. Calibrating on synthetic sequences with known ground truth, it detects an exact copy, a single point mutation, and a window shifted by up to $10\%$ of its length at every length we use; but at $70$\,bp it already misses two point mutations $70\%$ of the time, where at $300$\,bp and above it tolerates five. It is a near-exact-duplicate detector on short sequences and a genuine homology detector on long ones, so every leak fraction we report is a lower bound whose tightness depends on length. Specificity needs care, because the census statistic is not a per-pair score: for each test sequence it takes a \emph{maximum} over every training sequence, so a per-pair false-positive rate would have to fall well below $1/n_{\text{train}}$ before the resulting fraction were negligible. Pairwise sampling cannot settle that---bounding the per-pair rate tightly enough for a training set of $47{,}356$ would take on the order of $10^{7}$ pairs---so we do not argue it from pairs. We report instead what the statistic reads on data whose cleanliness is established without reference to it. Two such sources exist here. The first is construction: in the fix-a-leaky manipulation of \S\ref{sec:r-manip} every duplicated coordinate was collapsed \emph{by the edit itself}, so the merged arm is clean as a property of what we did to it rather than of what the estimator says, and it reads $0.012$ on $81{,}868$ sequences; the dose-zero control of \S\ref{sec:r-dose} is the same kind of datapoint, reading $0.004$ on $20{,}000$. The second is provenance: four of the five clean Genomic Benchmarks datasets have a documented merge-and-deduplicate step and the fifth, \textit{demo\_human\_or\_worm}, is a cross-species disjoint draw; none carries a material share of rows on duplicated coordinates, the largest being that fifth dataset at $0.04\%$ against \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}'s $94.3\%$. That is evidence in coordinate space, independent of any $k$-mer, and the statistic reads $0.001$ to $0.016$ across them. Against those, the two leaky datasets read $0.384$ and $0.406$. The floor of this estimator on real DNA at realistic training-set size is therefore an order of magnitude below the verdict cut and more than twentyfold below the leaky readings.

We are explicit that the GUE tasks cannot join that list. Their ``clean'' label \emph{is} this estimator's output, so quoting their leak fractions as evidence about the estimator's floor would be circular. What the GUE rows do supply independently is the exact-duplicate rate, computed by string equality on raw sequences with no $k$-mers and no threshold: across the eleven predicted-leaky tasks it runs $0.000$ to $0.026$, agreeing with the $k$-mer verdicts through an entirely separate code path.

Two further properties of the estimator are worth recording. Its per-pair behaviour on real DNA is much heavier-tailed than on synthetic sequence---random pairs drawn from the clean datasets reach a Jaccard of $0.26$, against a maximum of $0.019$ over uniform random sequence, because real genomes carry repeats and low-complexity tracts---so a synthetic null would materially understate it. And the containment index is uniformly the more sensitive of the two metrics at short length, which is why we report both rather than treat the second as redundant.

Second, the detector cannot distinguish a curation defect from biology. The GUE \textit{virus\_covid} task, which we registered as clean, measures a near-duplicate fraction of $1.000$---every test sequence has a near-duplicate in training, the largest value we observed anywhere. It is not a curation defect. The task is nine-way SARS-CoV-2 variant classification over $999$\,bp windows of a ${\sim}30$\,kb genome whose variants differ by a handful of mutations, so the test-to-train similarity has a \emph{minimum} of $0.824$ and a median of $0.987$: the corpus is near-identical by biology, and no split of it could be otherwise. A near-duplicate leak fraction carries no information there. Any application of this audit to viral, organellar, or other highly conserved sequence sets must establish that distinction by other means---the coordinate-space signature of \S\ref{sec:m-coord} is one, since biological conservation does not produce duplicated interval coordinates.

Three limits bound the study's scope, and the largest is a deliberate exclusion we can quantify. The pretrained foundation models often expected in such a comparison---DNABERT-2 \citep{zhou2023dnabert2}, HyenaDNA \citep{nguyen2023hyenadna}, and the Nucleotide Transformer \citep{dallatorre2024nucleotide}---are both GPU-gated and, more fundamentally, contaminated by construction. The two leaky datasets' sequences are extracted from the human reference genome---we recovered their hg38 chromosomal coordinates to build the chromosome-holdout control (\S\ref{sec:chrom})---so \emph{all} of these test sequences lie within HyenaDNA's whole-genome (hg38) pretraining corpus and within the human component of DNABERT-2's and the Nucleotide Transformer's multispecies corpora; the pretraining-overlap is thus $\approx100\%$, not a quantity we must estimate. Fine-tuning a model that has already seen the test sequences during pretraining opens a second leakage channel that no train/test re-split can close, so any corrected-split drop it produced would be a confounded lower bound rather than split-effect evidence. We therefore scope these models out and name---and here quantify---the confound rather than absorb it. Whether the foundation models practitioners actually deploy inherit this inflation is a distinct and important question that we leave \emph{genuinely open}: answering it requires an evaluation protocol that accounts for pretraining overlap, itself an instance of the leakage problem we study, and our from-scratch CNN establishes only that the mechanism reaches a trained neural network with no pretraining confound. Containment and alignment identity give lower bounds on leakage, and the chromosome-holdout control leaves a small residual. Natural extensions are a pretraining-overlap-aware foundation-model evaluation, additional suites, and additional biological domains (protein, long-range regulatory). DeepSTARR \citep{dealmeida2022deepstarr}, BEND \citep{marin2024bend} and DART-Eval \citep{patel2024darteval} are the obvious next targets. We state the basis for that expectation carefully, having just had the same expectation falsified: it is not that they are short-sequence regulatory benchmarks---eleven GUE tasks meeting exactly that description, seven of them independent, are clean (\S\ref{sec:r-census})---but that each assembles its examples from genome-tiled or annotation-overlapping intervals, which is the construction step that produced the defect here. That is a prediction about how a dataset was built, not about what it contains, and it is the one our evidence actually supports.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Near-duplicate leakage in a widely used genomic benchmark suite does more than inflate scores: on one of its datasets it changes which model the benchmark reports as best. Two of the seven binary Genomic Benchmarks datasets are leaky, one is borderline under a length-robust metric, and four are clean, so the problem is dataset-specific rather than systemic. On the two leaky datasets a near-duplicate-aware re-split costs the best model $11.8$ points on \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters} and $16.5$ on \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl}, with every leaky drop interval excluding zero and every clean-dataset delta including it. On \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} the leading model changes under accuracy, AUROC and $F_1$, under a chromosome-holdout control, under an alignment-scored re-clustering, and under a bootstrap winner probability; \textit{human\_nontata\_promoters}, where the demotion is accuracy-only and the clusters chain heavily, we report as a cautionary partial case rather than a second demonstration.

Three findings give that result its weight. On \textit{human\_enhancers\_ensembl} a nine-learner roster shows the damage is not an artifact of a small comparison set: the leaky split cannot separate its top three models---they finish within $0.0015$---yet those same three span $23$ accuracy points once corrected, so the benchmark is blind to real differences between models it calls equal. The cause on that dataset is a curation defect rather than a modelling choice, visible in the shipped genomic coordinates as $77{,}421$ positive intervals on $40{,}934$ distinct positions, and editing that construction step alone both abolishes the reordering there and manufactures it in a clean dataset. And a cross-suite census finds three of eleven Nucleotide Transformer tasks leaky---one by byte-identical duplication, two by cross-species splice-site homology---with no material ranking inversion on any of them, a null our own diagnostic condition explains in advance, because reordering additionally requires a challenger that is genuinely better on novel sequences.

Four practices follow for anyone building or using such a suite. Audit at full dataset scale, since subsampling can make a heavily leaky dataset look clean. Report both a length-blind and a length-robust similarity, since the containment index flagged as borderline one dataset the $k$-mer Jaccard scored clean. Deduplicate in coordinate space before splitting, since the defect here was introduced upstream of any sequence-level analysis and is plainly visible there. And include at least one memorization-prone learner in any comparison, because the gap between its leaky and corrected scores is a direct tripwire for a contaminated split---costlier than the as-shipped screens above, since it requires building the corrected split, but diagnostic of the consequence rather than of the contamination alone. The report card, the dependency-free near-duplicate-aware splitter, and the certification tool are released so that a new dataset can be checked before it becomes a leaderboard. The reordering result itself remains a single-dataset existence proof, scoped to comparisons containing a memorization-prone model; what generalizes is the audit.
